# Gap Research: What People Actually Wish For

This file documents the real research used to find and prioritize gaps in this skill's domain coverage (see `extending.md` for the general "how to source a domain file" process — this is that process's output, not a new mechanism). Pulled via live web/Perplexity search, not invented. Use it to sanity-check whether a wish's category is already well-covered, and to justify *why* a given domain file exists at the priority it does.

## The primary source: The Real Wishes Report (Real Insurance / CoreData, February 2020)

A real quantitative survey of 5,000 Australians (18+), commissioned by Real Insurance and conducted by CoreData in October 2019. Not a small or informal poll — this is the closest thing found to a direct "what do people actually wish for" dataset with real category-level scoring (each category scored 0–100 as a "wishes index").

**Full source material is kept locally, not just summarized here:** `references/sources/real-wishes-report-2020.txt` (plain-text extraction of the full report, ~1,090 lines) and `references/sources/real-wishes-report-2020.pdf` (the original document, fetched from `realinsurance.com.au/documents/real-wishes-report-2020-whitepaper.pdf`). If this file's summary ever seems to be missing a category breakdown, a generational stat, or a specific number, check the `.txt` extraction before re-fetching — it has the full report, including sections not summarized below (e.g. per-state/territory breakdowns of travel wishes).

**The six wish categories, by index score (higher = stronger wish/desire):**

| Category | Index score | Top items within it |
|---|---|---|
| Experience | 64.3 (highest) | Try new things (70.2), pursue hobbies/interests (69.4), travel bucket list (67.3), follow passion (66.4), become good at something (62.7), become less materialistic (50.7) |
| Family & Friends | 62.0 | Spend more time with loved ones (81.2), be present when needed most (80.7), keep family happy/together (79.6), find/keep true love (73.3), stay in touch with old friends (55.1), reconcile with someone (30.5) |
| Health & Wellbeing | 57.3 | Make health a priority (76.5), seek personal growth/happiness (66.9), control stress (61.5), trust one's gut (60.0), embrace JOMO (57.6) |
| Financial | 51.5 | Build financial security (73.9), budget/save better (71.1), splurge on loved ones (52.1), buy a dream property (50.2) |
| Career | 41.8 (tied lowest) | Work-life balance (51.5), job security (51.2), update/retrain skills (49.1), find dream job (46.9), start own business (29.1, lowest item in category) |
| Environmental & Global | 41.8 (tied lowest) | Help the environment (58.1), invest in sustainability (53.9), alleviate poverty (49.1), leave a legacy (32.3), take up a political/social cause (30.4) |

**Generational pattern worth remembering:** career and environmental/global wishes show the steepest generational gradient (near-zero for people over 55, quite high for Gen Z) — these read as youth-skewed wish categories, not universal ones. Family and health wishes are comparatively stable across generations.

**"Would you rather" tradeoff data (top 6 answers)** — this is the empirical backing now used in `da-rules.md` category 6 instead of an unsupported assertion:
- 87.5% would rather be the most content/satisfied person in the world than the richest/most powerful.
- 84.1% would rather be happy but not well-liked, than well-liked but not happy.
- 79.9% would rather have perfect health with few friends than many friends with poor health.
- 78.6% would rather be exceptionally intelligent but less attractive, than the reverse.
- 78.3% would rather be the smartest person in the world than the most attractive.
- 59.0% would rather live to 75 in perfect health than to 100 with health issues.

**"I wish I never" — regret data (top 5, multiple answers allowed):** worried what others thought of me so much (46.5%), sweated the small stuff (41.8%), lost contact with old friends (41.4%), took my health for granted (38.9%), failed to follow my true passion (30.1%).

**Barriers to making wishes come true (top answers):** affordability (53.5%), self-discipline/motivation (28.7%), fear of defeat/lack of confidence (27.5%), health (26.9%), finding energy (22.8%), family demands (22.3%), finding time (21.9%), waiting to "feel ready" (20.1%), not knowing exactly what to do (20.0%). This is the empirical basis for treating "waiting to feel ready" and "not knowing what to do" as distinct resource-gap sub-patterns in `intent-axes.md`, alongside the original plan/resource/structural split.

**Support matters:** 48.9% of respondents credit a partner, friend, or family member's encouragement as what got them moving on an important wish — backs up why a Redirect toward an accountability structure (not just a plan) is often the right second half of a Direct Grant.

## Cross-validating source: Bronnie Ware's hospice regrets

From *The Top Five Regrets of the Dying* (2012), based on Ware's palliative-care work: (1) wishing they'd had the courage to live true to themselves rather than the life others expected, (2) wishing they hadn't worked so hard, (3) wishing they'd had the courage to express their feelings, (4) wishing they'd stayed in touch with friends, (5) wishing they'd let themselves be happier.

**Why this matters methodologically:** two independent sources — a 5,000-person consumer survey and decades of hospice-patient testimony — converge on the same two themes: *staying connected to people* and *living authentically / following one's own path rather than others' expectations* are among the most commonly regretted gaps, more so than money or achievement. That convergence is why `relationships.md` was treated as the highest-priority missing domain file (this skill previously had zero content for the "family and friends" wish category despite it being tied for the single highest-scoring wish category in real data).

## Secondary sources (used for the true-desire taxonomy already in `intent-axes.md`, not for new domain files)

- NBER Working Paper 33846, "What Do People Want?" (Benjamin et al., 2025): large-scale stated-preference study finding family, money, and health as the three aspects with the highest marginal utility — broadly consistent with the Real Wishes Report's top categories.
- Steven Reiss's 16 Basic Desires (Ohio State, 1998): a taxonomy of intrinsic motivations (curiosity, honor, independence, power, social contact, vengeance, etc.) — useful background on individual variation in what drives people, but not used to restructure the true-desire table since Self-Determination Theory's smaller five-node version (`intent-axes.md`) is deliberately kept small enough to actually apply per-wish.

## Theoretical grounding: what a "wish" actually is

Everything above is empirical (what people say they wish for). This section is different: it's the academic grounding for treating a "wish" as a legitimate, well-defined psychological object worth building a whole skill around, rather than just borrowed genie flavor text.

**Source, read in full:** Glăveanu, V. P. (2023). "Wish." In *The Palgrave Encyclopedia of the Possible* (pp. 1763–1772). Palgrave Macmillan, Cham. DOI: 10.1007/978-3-030-90913-0_179. Full text extracted to `references/sources/` is not kept here (the source book is 1,812 pages; only the ~10-page entry was read, directly from the user's local copy, via `pdftotext -f 1790 -l 1799`).

**The core validating claim:** wishes are not signs of irrationality or pathological thinking — Glăveanu argues (against both the psychoanalytic and cognitive-bias traditions) for a sociocultural account: wish-making is "part and parcel of everyday life," a normal way people engage with and try to shape the future. This is the academic version of this skill's own premise — a wish deserves to be taken seriously and decoded, not waved off as a fantasy.

**The claim that most directly validates this skill's architecture:** a wish sits in a genuinely distinct conceptual space — "not as general as desires or needs, and yet not as specific as intentions or goals." It's this in-between-ness (vague enough to need interpretation, specific enough to be about something real) that makes intent-axes.md's whole approach necessary — a wish is definitionally underspecified in a way a goal or an intention isn't, so translating it into one is the actual work, not an optional extra step.

**A real, concrete technique found via this reading, not previously in this skill:** Glăveanu's entry cites Johannessen, Oettingen & Mayer (2012), a study on "mentally contrasting" a dieting wish against present reality/constraints, which produced measurably better follow-through than indulging in the imagined future alone. This is Gabriele Oettingen's mental-contrasting/WOOP research program — a well-established, decades-deep body of real psychology on turning a wish specifically (not a goal, not an intention) into committed action. It's now a tenet in `references/domains/psychology.md`, and it's arguably the single most directly on-topic piece of research this skill has incorporated, since it's literally about the wish-to-action mechanism this whole skill exists to shortcut.

**Other findings from the same entry, not built into a tenet (noted for completeness):** real research on wishful thinking as sometimes *adaptive* rather than purely biased — Neuman, Rafferty & Griffiths (2014) found a bias toward optimism can improve decision-making specifically when computing all outcome probabilities is intractable (bounded rationality), which is a useful nuance against treating every optimistic wish as an error to correct. Also: real wish-fulfillment research on children with life-threatening illness (Ewing 2009; Darlington et al. 2013; Galinsky 2014) found that granting a specific wish gives the child "a sense of the future" and eases suffering — a genuine empirical echo of this skill's premise that fulfilling a wish well is not a frivolous act.

### The psychoanalytic paradigm: Pataki's philosophical account of wish-fulfilment, and where Simon's power paper does (and doesn't) fit

Glăveanu's entry mentions Freudian wish-fulfilment only secondhand, in passing, as a tradition he's arguing *against* (in favor of the sociocultural account above). It doesn't actually explain what wish-fulfilment is claimed to be, mechanically, or engage with the philosophical literature that has scrutinized Freud's own conception since. Four sources were read directly (via this environment's avangarde-rag knowledge base, targeted `search_knowledge_base` queries rather than a linear read, given one of them — the Pataki book — is 899 chunks) to fill that gap:

- Tamas Pataki, *Wish-fulfilment in Philosophy and Psychoanalysis: The Tyranny of Desire* (Routledge, Psychoanalytic Explorations series) — a full book-length philosophical treatment.
- Tamas Pataki, "Intention in Wish-fulfilment" (*Australasian Journal of Philosophy*, 1996) — the earlier journal-article version of the book's central argument.
- Tamas Pataki, "Self-deception and Wish-fulfilment" (*Philosophia*, 1997) — extends the same framework to self-deception specifically.
- Bennett Simon, "The Power of the Wish and the Wish for Power: A Discussion" (*Psychoanalytic Inquiry*, 1986).

**Pataki's central move: "ordinary wish-fulfilment" and "Freudian wish-fulfilment" (FWT) are not the same concept, and Freud himself ran them together.** Ordinary wish-fulfilment (wishing it would stop raining, and it does, and you notice, and you stop wishing) requires that the wish be *extinguished* (the agent ceases to wish that p — a conceptual condition, not just an epistemic one: a wish can look extinguished while actually repressed) **and** that the extinction be a genuine *causal consequence* of the real state of affairs obtaining — not merely coincide with it. Pataki's test case: if you're deceived into believing you won the lottery and the wish to win the lottery duly extinguishes, that isn't wish-fulfilment in the ordinary sense, even though the wish did extinguish, because the belief wasn't caused by an actual win. Freudian wish-fulfilment (FWT) is a different, "singular" thing: the wish is quieted not by the real object but by an *imaginative or hallucinatory representation* of its fulfilment (a dream, a fantasy, a symptom) — what Freud calls "substitutive satisfaction" and what Pataki, following Jim Hopkins, formalizes as producing "pacification without satisfaction": the wish's felt urgency drops even though the actual state of the world never changed.

**Pataki's most load-bearing philosophical claim, and the one most distinct from Glăveanu's account:** FWT is not adventitious or accidental. It is itself either intended — often *unconsciously* intended — or instrumental to some other, superordinate form of intended "self-solicitude": the self taking itself as an object of care and concern (self-consolation, self-appeasement, sustaining self-esteem, regulating painful affect). This is a genuinely different theoretical register from Glăveanu's sociocultural account of wishing as ordinary future-oriented meaning-making — Pataki isn't arguing wishes are pathological, but he is arguing that *some* wish-satisfaction happens entirely inside the head, on purpose (if unconsciously), and that this is worth telling apart from wish-satisfaction that changes anything real. The two accounts complement rather than contradict each other: Glăveanu explains why having and voicing wishes is a normal, healthy part of everyday cognition; Pataki explains the specific mechanism by which a wish can go quiet without ever being met.

**The self-deception paper adds a workable technical criterion, not just a link between two folk concepts.** Pataki sets out minimal conditions for *strong* self-deception: to be self-deceived about p, an agent must (1) believe p, (2) intentionally deceive themselves into believing p, (3) hold that belief despite it being epistemically unwarranted, (4) have that belief causally sustained by a contemporaneous desire, and (5) *simultaneously* believe not-p. That last condition is the interesting one: strong self-deception, on this account, is not simple ignorance or honest optimism — it's a live, held contradiction, sustained by wanting p to be true. Pataki's conclusion is that much of what gets called self-deception is in the strategic service of FWT — used for self-consolation, self-appeasement, or protecting self-esteem, the same motives that drive wish-fulfilment generally.

**What became a tenet, and what stayed here as grounding only:** the ordinary/FWT distinction and its "pacification without satisfaction" mechanism, plus the self-deception contemporaneous-contradiction test, were concrete and observable enough to become a new tenet in `references/domains/psychology.md` (cross-referenced from `da-rules.md` category 6) — see that file for the exact runtime-usable version. What stayed here, deliberately not built into a tenet: Pataki's full six-way representational taxonomy of a wish (wish / representation of wish / object that would fulfil it / representation of that object / representation of the wish-as-fulfilled / the fulfilment itself as process-vs-end-state), and the extended debate with Hopkins, Johnston, and McLaughlin over whether wish-fulfilling belief-formation is "intentional" or "sub-intentional" (i.e., whether it requires a literal, if unconscious, act of the will, or is better modelled as a lower-level, non-agentive mental regularity). Both are real and central to Pataki's argument, but they're a philosophy-of-mind dispute about the *architecture* of intention, not something this skill can act on mid-conversation — forcing that distinction into a runtime tenet would be cramming jargon in for its own sake.

**Simon's "wish for power" paper turned out to be a different kind of document than expected, and didn't clear the bar for a new tenet.** It isn't a standalone theory of wish-for-power; it's Simon's discussant essay introducing and critiquing five other papers submitted to a *Psychoanalytic Inquiry* issue on power and psychoanalysis (Person on male sexuality and power, Grotstein on the psychology of powerlessness, a paper on the "imposter" problem in analytic training, and others on psychohistory/Hitler). Simon's own original contributions are more diagnostic-of-a-field than mechanism-of-a-wish: "lack, not plenty, motivates inquiry" (those without a kind of power are the ones who write about it), and a claim that psychoanalysts themselves, largely lacking real political/economic power, seek "derivative, symbolic, and wish-fulfilling" substitutes for it — vicarious power through identification with great writers or historical figures, or through exercising power within their own professional institutes. This is a real, on-topic instance of a status/power wish standing in for something else — but it doesn't add a *mechanism* beyond what Housel's ego-vs-income framing and Branden's Six Pillars already cover in `psychology.md`'s category-6 tenet: it's corroborating evidence, from a third intellectual tradition, for the same substitution pattern, not a new decision-usable move. It's recorded here rather than duplicated as a tenet for that reason.

## Broadening beyond a single-country survey: Pew's 17-economy "What Makes Life Meaningful"

The Real Wishes Report is one country (Australia), one year (2020) — a real limitation flagged in this file since it was written. The best available broadening found: Pew Research Center, *"What Makes Life Meaningful? Views From 17 Advanced Economies"* (2021) — 18,850 adults surveyed across Canada, the US, and 15 other advanced economies (Belgium, France, Germany, Greece, Italy, Netherlands, Spain, Sweden, UK, Australia, Japan, New Zealand, Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan), via an open-ended "what makes your life feel meaningful" question, hand-coded into 20 categories. Different phrasing from "what do you wish for" (meaning vs. wish), but close enough in spirit to serve as a genuine cross-cultural check on the Real Wishes Report's six categories — and no direct wish-phrased successor at this scale was found (checked; none exists publicly as of this search).

**What it confirms:** family is the dominant top category in 14 of 17 economies (50%+ in Australia, New Zealand, Greece, the US) — consistent with the Real Wishes Report's "family & friends" scoring tied for highest. Career/work and material wellbeing both place top-3 in most countries, also consistent.

**What it corrects — the single most important addition from this pass:** the six-category picture in this file is not universal, it's a broadly Western/Anglophone pattern, and Pew's data shows real, substantial cross-cultural variation this file previously had no way to flag:
- **Taiwan** ranks *society/institutions* — not family — as the #1 source of meaning, the only economy surveyed where this happens.
- **South Korea** ranks *material wellbeing* #1, while career/work drops to just 6% (versus 43% in Italy) — the widest swing in the entire dataset on a single category.
- **The US** is the only economy where religion places in the top 5 (15%); everywhere else it's 5% or less.
- Younger respondents (under 30) skew toward friends, education, and hobbies over family/career — a generational pattern independently consistent with the Real Wishes Report's own age-gradient findings for career and environmental wishes.

**Practical implication:** the domain files in this skill (`finance.md`, `career.md`, `relationships.md`, etc.) are grounded in data that reflects Australian/broadly-Western respondents specifically. If a wish comes from a user whose cultural context is closer to Taiwan's (institutions/society-oriented) or South Korea's (material-security-oriented, low work-centrality) than to the Anglophone pattern, treat the domain files' resource-gap tables as a reasonable default, not a universal law — the underlying mechanisms (habit loops, WOOP, NVC) are more culture-general than the *priority weighting* of which wish-categories matter most.

## A large real study checked and correctly not used: the Global Flourishing Study

Worth knowing about, and worth knowing why it isn't cited above as a data source. The Global Flourishing Study (Gallup + Harvard/Baylor, 200,000+ participants, 22 countries, annual longitudinal waves) is real, large, current, and measures six dimensions — happiness/life satisfaction, health, meaning, character, relationships, financial stability — that map almost one-to-one onto this skill's own domain-file categories (a nice independent structural validation that the domain split is a reasonable one). But it measures *existing conditions and satisfaction*, not aspirational wishes or ranked life priorities — there's no "what do you wish for" or "what matters most to you" item in its design. It answers "how are people doing," not "what do people want that they don't have," which is the question this skill actually needs answered. Correctly excluded as a direct source; worth re-checking if its question design changes in a future wave.
