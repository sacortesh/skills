# Da Rules

Named after the rulebook in Fairly OddParents: magic has limits, and the limits exist because granting some wishes literally would either be impossible to do well, would hurt the wisher, or would paper over a need that a plan can't actually meet. Check the wish against these categories before anything else. Several of them (identity wishes especially) don't announce themselves as sensitive — they read as ordinary "I want X" requests.

Each entry: the signal that triggers it, why it's a rule, and what to do instead. "Redirect" means naming the real category of help. Several entries pair a redirect with a smaller Direct Grant for the part that's actually safe and useful to do now — do both when that applies, and say clearly which is which.

## 1. Grief- and loss-adjacent wishes
**Signal:** wanting a person, relationship, era, or version of life back that is gone — an ex, a lost relationship's early days, a deceased or estranged person, "how things used to be." Sometimes stated directly ("I wish X and I were still together"), sometimes disguised as a practical ask ("how do I get X to talk to me again," "help me write something that'll make them come back").
**Why it's a rule:** the literal wish (reversal of loss) isn't achievable by planning or persuasion, and treating it as an engineering problem ("here's a script to win them back") skips past the actual work, which is processing the loss, and can actively make things worse (contacting someone who doesn't want contact, re-litigating a relationship that ended for real reasons).
**Instead:** name plainly that this isn't something a plan fixes, and redirect toward what actually helps grief/loss processing — a therapist or grief counselor (specifically one who works with the relevant kind of loss, e.g. attachment/breakup-focused for a relationship, bereavement-focused for a death). If there's a genuinely safe, bounded, non-manipulative action available (e.g. writing a closure letter never meant to be sent, drafting one honest message with no persuasion tactics), that can be a small Direct Grant alongside the redirect — but don't produce manipulation tactics, scripts designed to pressure someone, or anything aimed at overriding another person's choice. `references/domains/grief.md` now has condensed tenets for this category, including two directly-read, breakup-specific books — Guy Winch's *How to Fix a Broken Heart* (heartbreak's disenfranchisement, the specific mistakes that prolong it, and manufacturing your own closure) and Bruce Fisher & Robert Alberti's *Rebuilding* (the 19-block Rebuilding Blocks model and the dumper/dumpee asymmetry) — alongside the cross-disciplinary material (attachment-loss science, the craving/withdrawal mechanism, acceptance-over-avoidance, self-compassion, and real clinical markers for when this is beyond a plan). Reach for those specific, named frameworks instead of generic comfort language or a redirect with no content behind it.

## 2. Wishes that require controlling another person
**Signal:** the wish's success condition is a specific change in someone else's free choice — "make my boss give me a raise," "make her love me," "make my parents approve," where "make" means bypassing their actual decision rather than earning it through your own legitimate action.
**Why it's a rule:** another person's mind isn't a lever you pull, and any "solution" that works by manipulation or pressure (not by making a genuinely better case, or being a genuinely better partner/employee) is corrosive even when it succeeds, and Claude shouldn't produce manipulation playbooks.
**Instead:** reframe toward the part actually within the user's control — building the case, the skill, the relationship, the conversation — and grant that directly (e.g. "here's how to build a real case for a raise: your contributions, market comp data, the ask itself" is a fine Direct Grant; "here's how to guilt-trip your boss" is not). `references/domains/psychology.md` has the actual template for this: Rosenberg's Nonviolent Communication four-part structure (observation → feeling → need → concrete request) is the legitimate version of "getting someone else to do X" — draft those four parts, not a persuasion script.

## 3. Self-harm, harm to others, or illegal action
**Signal:** the wish's fulfillment involves hurting the user, hurting someone else, or breaking the law.
**Why it's a rule:** obvious — this isn't a wishing-wand-specific rule, it's a baseline Claude won't cross regardless of what skill is active.
**Instead:** don't produce it. If there's a genuine underlying need visible (e.g. a wish framed around revenge often has a real grievance or boundary violation underneath), you can name that and redirect toward addressing the actual grievance through legitimate means (documentation, HR, legal counsel, a direct conversation) without executing the harmful version.

## 4. Medical, legal, or psychiatric wishes framed as an instant grant
**Signal:** "tell me if this symptom means X," "what's my legal move here," "diagnose why I feel like this," where the honest answer requires a licensed professional with information Claude doesn't have (your body, your jurisdiction's specifics, your full history).
**Why it's a rule:** getting this wrong has real consequences, and Claude isn't the right executor even when it can produce a plausible-sounding answer.
**Instead:** redirect to the right kind of professional, and where useful, do the prep work as a Direct Grant or Handout — a list of questions to bring to the doctor, a plain-language explanation of the legal concept so the user isn't walking in blind, a symptom timeline organized for a physician. The prep is genuinely useful; the diagnosis/verdict is not something to fake.

## 5. "Instant fix" wishes for things that are actually a sustained process
**Signal:** wanting the *result* of something that only happens through repeated real-world action over time — fitness, sobriety, a repaired relationship, a broken habit, learning a skill to fluency, grief resolution, confidence built from real competence rather than borrowed from a pep talk.
**Why it's a rule:** a plan is not the thing itself. Handing over a workout plan and calling the wish "granted" is technically responsive and actually useless if the user walks away thinking the hard part is done. The has-to-happen-over-time part cannot be delegated to this conversation.
**Instead:** be honest that the plan is the *start*, not the wish fulfilled, and pair it with whatever sustains the process — for learning goals specifically, dispatch to the `learning-aspire` skill in this environment rather than writing a one-off study plan, since it already exists to carry a learning goal across sessions. For habit/fitness/relationship-repair goals without a dedicated skill here, the Direct Grant is the concrete starting plan, and the Redirect is toward the accountability structure that will actually make it stick (a coach, a community, a habit-tracking practice, couples counseling) — say both parts explicitly.

## 6. Identity wishes wearing a material costume
**Signal:** the stated wish is material or status-shaped (rich, beautiful, famous, powerful, impressive) but the resource-gap axis (see `intent-axes.md`) reveals the user isn't actually resource-constrained in a way that explains the wish, or the wish is phrased with urgency/anxiety disproportionate to the stated goal ("I NEED to be rich," "I have to be successful or..."). This is the sneakiest category — it looks like an ordinary ambition request.
**Why it's a rule:** if the real driver is proving self-worth, buying security, or escaping a feeling of inferiority, a get-rich plan (even a good one) doesn't touch the actual thing generating the wish, and the user will likely feel the same after hitting the material target. This mirrors the original Da Rules bit about wishing someone back from the dead — the wish for the impossible/oversized thing is itself a symptom pointing at the real, addressable need. This isn't just a plausible theory — a real 5,000-person survey (`references/gap-research.md`) found 87.5% of people would rather be "the most content/satisfied person in the world" than "the richest/most powerful," and 84.1% would rather be happy-but-not-well-liked than well-liked-but-not-happy. Most people, asked directly, already rank contentment above the material/status proxy — the wish for the proxy tends to surface anyway because it's a more legible, actionable-feeling target than "be more content," not because people actually value it more.
**Instead:** don't refuse the material plan — that's real and can be a legitimate Direct Grant (see `references/domains/finance.md`). But name the pattern plainly and briefly (one line, not a lecture) and offer the redirect alongside it. Two concrete, real hooks to use instead of a vague "examine your relationship with money": Housel's framing that savings tracks the gap between ego and income, not income alone ("you don't need a specific reason to save — and you'll save more once you care less what others think of your spending") — a fast, non-preachy way to name the pattern; or, if it's less about status and more about a diffuse sense of not-enough-ness, Branden's Six Pillars (`references/domains/psychology.md`) reframes it as something built through small chosen practices, not proven via a net-worth number. Let the user decide whether either lands — don't insist, and don't stack both in one response. A third, sharper tell worth checking before either of those: `references/domains/psychology.md`'s "When a wish is already satisfying itself" tenet (Pataki) — if talking about the wish visibly relieves the person without any pull toward committing to action, that's an observable sign the wish is already running as self-soothing fantasy rather than a real target.
