# Psychology Domain Tenets

Condensed from books actually read and indexed in this environment's library (Clear, Fogg, Duhigg, Branden, Rubin, Rosenberg, and the CBT-for-social-anxiety clinical literature), plus one foundational mechanism sourced via live research (Oettingen — see below). These are the mechanisms behind most of the wishes this skill sees — habit-shaped wishes, self-worth-shaped wishes, and control-another-person wishes each have a real, well-established framework; use the matching one instead of generic encouragement.

## The universal mechanism: mental contrasting turns a wish into a plan (Gabriele Oettingen's WOOP — verified via live research, not in this environment's indexed library)

Before reaching for any domain-specific framework below, this is the general-purpose mechanism this skill's whole flow is an informal version of. Oettingen's research (encountered via Johannessen, Oettingen & Mayer's 2012 dieting-wish study, cited in Glăveanu's 2023 "Wish" encyclopedia entry — see `references/gap-research.md`'s theoretical-grounding section) identifies what actually separates a wish that turns into action from one that stays a fantasy: **mental contrasting**, not positive thinking alone. The structure, WOOP: **W**ish (specific, vivid) → **O**utcome (the best realistic result of achieving it, imagined concretely) → **O**bstacle (the *specific internal* obstacle standing in the way — in oneself, not circumstances) → **P**lan (an if-then implementation intention tied directly to that obstacle: "if [obstacle happens], then I will [response]").

The load-bearing finding: contrasting the wish against present reality — naming the real internal obstacle — before planning produces measurably more committed, effective action (Johannessen et al.'s participants who contrasted a dieting wish against their actual constraints ate less and moved more over the following weeks); indulging in the imagined future *without* contrasting it against an obstacle does not, and can actively relax someone out of acting, because vividly imagining success gets partially mistaken by the mind for already having achieved it.

Practical implication for this skill: when a wish reads as a fantasy with no obstacle named ("I want to be rich," "I want to be more confident," "I want to get in shape"), the single highest-leverage clarifying move is surfacing the *specific internal obstacle* — not jumping straight to an external plan. This is a distinct question from the resource-gap axis (`intent-axes.md`): the resource-gap axis asks what's missing externally; WOOP's Obstacle step asks what the person's own likely failure point will be once the external resources are already in place. A Direct Grant is strongest when it visibly does both — external plan plus the specific internal if-then for the moment they're most likely to derail.

## Habit-shaped wishes (sustained-process wishes, Da Rules category 5)

The shared mechanism across Clear, Fogg, and Duhigg is the **habit loop**: cue → craving → routine/response → reward, repeating until the brain stops deliberating and just runs it. Three practical levers, from most to least load-bearing when someone has already failed at this before:

1. **Shrink the behavior until motivation is irrelevant** (Fogg's Tiny Habits, B = MAP: Behavior happens when Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt converge — if ability is high enough, i.e. the behavior is tiny enough, you don't need much motivation at all). Concrete recipe format: "After I \_\_\_ [existing routine/anchor], I will \_\_\_ [new tiny behavior]," followed by immediate self-celebration. This is the fix for "I keep starting and quitting" specifically — the past attempts were sized for a motivated day, not a bad one.
2. **Make it identity-based, not outcome-based** (Clear's Atomic Habits: "you don't rise to the level of your goals, you fall to the level of your systems," and habits that stick are the ones framed as "I am someone who X" rather than "I am trying to X"). Useful reframe line for a Direct Grant: don't hand someone a goal, hand them a system and a one-line identity to go with it.
3. **Diagnose the actual loop before redesigning it** (Duhigg: you can't change a habit you haven't decomposed into its cue/craving/routine/reward — an "implementation intention," i.e. a specific plan for the cue, is what actually re-engineers it, not general resolve).

**Match the accountability structure to the person, not just the habit** (Rubin's Four Tendencies: people respond to expectations differently — some need external accountability to follow through at all (Obligers), some resist any imposed structure and need to frame it as their own choice (Rebels/Questioners), some just need clarity and will follow both inner and outer expectations (Upholders)). If a wish already names a specific failure pattern ("I always quit after two weeks"), that's a clue about which tendency is in play — worth reflecting back rather than assuming everyone needs the same kind of structure.

## Self-worth / identity-shaped wishes (Da Rules category 6)

Branden's Six Pillars of Self-Esteem: living consciously, self-acceptance, self-responsibility, self-assertiveness, living purposefully, personal integrity. The load-bearing insight for this skill's purposes: self-esteem is built through accumulated small chosen actions (each pillar is a *practice*, not a trait you either have or don't) — which means a wish like "I want to be rich/impressive/confident" as a proxy for self-worth cannot be resolved by the material outcome alone, because the material outcome isn't one of the six practices. Naming one concrete practice ("start being self-responsible about X specifically") is more useful than naming the book.

Branden also names a real, specific failure mode worth recognizing: **"happiness anxiety"** — self-sabotage triggered by approaching a goal, because achieving it feels undeserved or threatens some story about oneself. If a wish's urgency seems to spike right as progress happens, or the person seems to court setbacks near a finish line, this is the pattern, and it's a legitimate thing to reflect back rather than just re-issuing the same plan louder.

## When a wish is already satisfying itself (Da Rules category 6, and any wish whose own premise the wisher's words contradict)

Pataki's philosophical account of wish-fulfilment (*Wish-fulfilment in Philosophy and Psychoanalysis*, 2014; "Intention in Wish-fulfilment," 1996; "Self-deception and Wish-fulfilment," 1997 — read directly via this environment's knowledge base, not secondhand through Glăveanu's encyclopedia entry above) draws a distinction worth having on hand: **ordinary wish-fulfilment** (the wish is satisfied because the real state of affairs obtains, and that's why the wishing stops) is a different thing from **Freudian wish-fulfilment ("FWT")** — a wish quieted by an imagined or represented fulfilment (a fantasy, a symptom, a piece of self-talk) that produces "pacification without satisfaction": the felt urgency drops even though nothing real changed. Pataki's load-bearing claim is that this isn't accidental — FWT is itself intended, often unconsciously, in service of *self-solicitude* (self-consolation, self-appeasement, protecting self-esteem, managing painful affect).

**Practical implication:** this is the actual mechanism behind the WOOP tenet's warning above (vividly imagining success can relax someone out of acting instead of prompting it) — Pataki names why it happens and what it's doing for the person. The runtime tell: if simply voicing, elaborating on, or fantasizing about the wish visibly relieves the person in the conversation itself, with no pull toward committing to anything concrete, that relief is itself evidence the wish may already be doing its job as fantasy — quieting itself via imagination rather than needing a plan. This is a sharper, more observable signal than the general "urgency disproportionate to the stated goal" pattern named in `da-rules.md` category 6 — it's something you can watch happen in the exchange, not just infer from tone.

**Self-deception overlay, for wishes whose stated premise the wisher's own words already contradict:** Pataki's self-deception paper gives a concrete, non-hand-wavy test. Strong self-deception requires the person to *simultaneously* hold p and not-p: some part of them already believes the wish's premise is false or unsupported, while another part keeps voicing or acting on the wish, and a live desire is what sustains the split (this is distinct from honest optimism or simply not knowing better). Concrete tell to reach for: when the user's own words, in the same conversation, already contain the fact that contradicts the wish's premise (e.g. a category-1 case — "I wish my ex would come back," said alongside "he's blocked me and is seeing someone else") — that co-presence, not just hopefulness, is the marker of self-deception in this technical sense. It's legitimate grounds to name the contradiction plainly, once, briefly, and without a "gotcha" tone, before responding to the wish on its stated terms — answering the wish as if the premise were intact just hands the self-deception a more elaborate vehicle to run in.

## Confidence / social-anxiety-shaped wishes

The evidence-based mechanism (per the CBT clinical literature) is **cognitive restructuring + exposure**: identify the distorted automatic thought, then repeatedly and gradually face the avoided situation rather than reasoning your way out of it in the abstract. The important caveat for this skill: this is real clinical territory (Da Rules category 4 adjacent) — fine to explain the mechanism and even suggest a starter exposure ladder as a bounded Direct Grant, but a formal diagnosis or a full treatment plan is a professional's job, not this conversation's.

## Wishes that require another person's cooperation (Da Rules category 2)

Rosenberg's Nonviolent Communication four-part structure is the legitimate version of "getting someone else to do/give/agree to something," as opposed to the manipulation Da Rules category 2 rules out: **Observation** (what happened, free of judgment) → **Feeling** (how it lands, without blaming) → **Need** (what value is at stake) → **Request** (a concrete, doable ask, not a demand — the other person can say no). This is the actual template for a Direct Grant when a wish is "make my boss give me a raise" or "get my roommate to respect my time" — draft the four parts, not a persuasion script.
