# Grief and Loss Domain Tenets

Condensed from two directly-read, breakup-specific sources plus real cross-disciplinary research. The backbone is now two dedicated breakup/divorce-recovery books, read directly rather than cited secondhand: Guy Winch's *How to Fix a Broken Heart* (TED Books — a psychologist's practice-grounded book specifically on romantic heartbreak and pet-loss grief) and Bruce Fisher & Robert Alberti's *Rebuilding: When Your Relationship Ends* (4th edition — the long-standing, 35+-year, million-copies-in-print divorce/breakup recovery workbook, built on Fisher's own validated Divorce Adjustment Scale and decades of running recovery seminars). Around that backbone sits the cross-disciplinary material this file originally shipped with (Bowlby's attachment research as summarized in Seligman's *Authentic Happiness* and Haidt's *The Happiness Hypothesis*, Hayes' *Get Out of Your Mind and Into Your Life*, Neff's *Self-Compassion*, Lyubomirsky's *The How of Happiness*, Nagoski's *Come as You Are*, and the clinical grief chapters of Nemeroff & Craighead's *Patient Guide to Mood and Anxiety Disorders*), plus Helen Fisher et al.'s peer-reviewed fMRI research on romantic love as reported in Alter's *Irresistible* and Kaplan & Marsh's *How Luck Happens*. (Note the two unrelated Fishers in this file: Helen Fisher, the romantic-love neuroscientist, and Bruce Fisher, the divorce-recovery-model author — no relation.) This file exists to fill the gap `da-rules.md` names explicitly: category 1 (grief- and loss-adjacent wishes) previously had no domain file backing it at all — just "redirect to a therapist." It started as cross-disciplinary-only; it now has direct, breakup-specific sourcing too.

## The Rebuilding Blocks (Fisher & Alberti's structural map of divorce/breakup recovery)

Bruce Fisher (EdD, a divorce therapist who founded the Family Relations Learning Center in Boulder, CO) built the **Rebuilding Blocks** model from running a ten-week divorce recovery seminar and testing it against his own **Fisher Divorce Adjustment Scale** — a validated instrument, still online at rebuilding.org/assessment. The model names **nineteen rebuilding blocks**, arranged as a mountain to climb rather than a strict checklist: Denial, Fear, Adaptation, Loneliness, Friendship, Guilt/Rejection, Grief, Anger, Letting Go, Self-Worth, Transition, Openness, Love, Trust, Relatedness, Sexuality, Singleness, Purpose, and Freedom. People don't move through them in lockstep — several get worked on at once, and it's normal to double back — but they're roughly ordered from initial shock toward full autonomy, and the model is meant to be used diagnostically ("which block am I stuck on right now"), not just descriptively. (One caveat: block 7, Grief, leans on Kübler-Ross's five stages internally — the same caveat already on this file's Bowlby section applies there specifically; the other eighteen blocks don't depend on that framework.)

The model's other load-bearing concept is the **dumper/dumpee asymmetry**: Fisher Divorce Adjustment Scale data consistently shows the person who didn't choose the breakup (the "dumpee") reports significantly more pain at the point of separation — especially anger and difficulty letting go — than the person who ended it (the "dumper"), because the dumper typically began emotionally detaching *before* leaving, while the dumpee is often still fully in love when the relationship ends. (Mutual-decision breakups score more like dumpers, but with less grief.) There's also a predictable **"divorce honeymoon"** phase in the first few months: the dumper acts guilty and over-accommodating, the dumpee stays hopeful and compliant hoping for reconciliation — until the dumper's guilt curdles into irritation/anger and the dumpee's hope curdles into rejection-driven anger, usually around the three-month mark.

**When to reach for this:** the 19 blocks are a legitimate, specific answer to "why does this still hurt in weird ways" or "am I making progress" — naming which block someone is stuck on (loneliness vs. self-worth vs. letting go, say) is more useful than a vague "give it time." The dumper/dumpee framework directly answers two recurring wish shapes: "why do they seem so much less devastated than me" (they started grieving before the breakup happened; this is a timing gap, not a caring gap) and "why do I feel so guilty even though I'm the one who ended it" (guilt is the dumper's normal opening emotion, not evidence you did something wrong).

## Heartbreak's disenfranchisement and the mistakes that prolong it (Winch)

Guy Winch's core observation, opening *How to Fix a Broken Heart*: romantic heartbreak (and the loss of a cherished pet, which he treats as sharing the same mechanism) gets systematically less social and institutional validation than "legitimate" losses like a death or a formal divorce — no bereavement leave, friends losing patience faster, the sufferer's own self-talk turning "why am I still this upset over a breakup" into an added layer of shame. The disenfranchisement compounds the original wound: people internalize the message that their pain isn't proportionate and hide it further instead of getting support.

Winch's more actionable contribution is a short list of specific, evidence-linked **mistakes that set recovery back**, distinct from ACT's generic "avoidance" framing already in this file:

- **Overtaxing your support system** — repeating the same unresolved ruminations out loud over and over ("why wasn't I good enough," "why did they lie") without acknowledging insight already reached. This is understandable short-term but reliably burns out the support the person most needs, at the exact point they need it.
- **Refusing a reasonable explanation and chasing a hidden one** — the mind assumes intense pain must have an equally dramatic cause, turning the person into an amateur detective interrogating a breakup that had an ordinary, already-stated reason. Winch's reframe: this feels like detective work but runs on the same reward circuitry as addiction — it's a craving, not an investigation.
- **Idealizing the person who broke your heart** — its own chapter in the book; a nostalgia-driven "grass is greener" distortion that directly prolongs longing by editing out why the relationship actually ended.
- **Reflexively checking their social media** — the compulsive-checking urge Winch treats as literally craving-shaped, the same territory as this file's existing Helen Fisher/craving section, but Winch's specific angle is that it *feels* like information-gathering when it's actually the compulsion itself.

**When to reach for this:** a wish shaped like "why can't my friends just listen anymore," "I keep checking their Instagram," or "I need to know why they really did it" — name the specific mistake pattern rather than a vague "stop dwelling on it," and pair it with the concrete replacement behavior (see closure, below) rather than just telling the person to stop.

## Manufacturing your own closure (Winch)

Winch cites research on romantic breakups finding that the single strongest predictor of timely, healthy adjustment is having *certainty about why the breakup happened* — but pursuing the ex for a fuller or more honest answer almost never delivers that certainty, and usually just reopens vulnerability. His actual technique: construct **your own best-guess explanation** — one that fits the known facts, the ex's actual personality and history, and the context of the breakup, chosen deliberately to leave your pride, dignity, and self-esteem intact rather than to punish yourself. This isn't self-deception; it's picking the true-enough account, from among the plausible ones, that lets the search end.

Winch also flags a specific line worth distinguishing: mild, passing self-doubt after a breakup ("I wish I were prettier," "what did I do wrong") is normal and self-limiting, but catastrophic, absolute self-condemning beliefs ("no one will ever want me," "I'll be alone forever") are a distinct, more concerning cognitive pattern — not yet a diagnosis, but a marker worth naming if it shows up.

**When to reach for this:** a wish shaped like "I need them to tell me why," "I keep replaying it looking for the real reason," or "I can't move on until I understand" — the honest Direct Grant is help building the person's *own* closing narrative, not a promise that the ex will eventually supply a better one. If what the person says drifts from "I feel unattractive right now" toward "no one will ever want me," that's the moment to gently flag the difference and, if it's severe, lean toward the redirect below.

## Attachment-loss mechanism (Bowlby's protest–despair–detachment, extended to adult romantic bonds)

Bowlby's original observations of children separated from caregivers found a three-stage sequence: **protest** (crying, active searching, refusal to accept the loss), **despair** (passive listlessness, withdrawal), then **detachment** (alienation from the lost attachment figure, renewed sociability with others). Hazan and Shaver's extension of attachment theory "from the cradle to the grave" found the same machinery runs on adult romantic partners, not just parents — a partner becomes a genuine attachment figure providing Nagoski's three components (safe haven, secure base, and the correlate **separation distress** when the person is gone). A review of how adults cope with a spouse's death or a long separation found the same sequence Bowlby saw in children: initial anxiety and panic, then lethargy and depression, then recovery *through* a real, biologically-grounded process of emotional detachment — not through willpower or a clever reframe. The same review found contact with close friends did comparatively little to blunt the pain, while renewed contact with parents helped more — worth naming if the wish's support system leans entirely on friends.

**When to reach for this:** a wish framed as "why can't I just get over this" or "I still want them back" — the honest answer is that this is a real attachment-bond breaking, not a mood to argue yourself out of, and it runs on its own timeline through those three stages. **Caveat:** don't reach for Kübler-Ross's five stages of grief (denial/anger/bargaining/depression/acceptance) as the framework — it's the version most people already know, but it was never based on research and researchers on grief generally no longer use it; Bowlby's protest–despair–detachment sequence is the one with actual empirical support behind it.

## Craving/withdrawal mechanism (Fisher's romantic-love neuroscience)

Helen Fisher and colleagues' fMRI studies of people in the early, intense stage of romantic love found dopamine-reward-system activation (in the ventral tegmental area) closely resembling the pattern seen in substance craving — the same circuitry that generates wanting, seeking, and craving, not just pleasure. Fisher's own framing: "love is like cocaine." The practical implication for loss: when that romantic attachment is cut off, what follows has real craving/withdrawal characteristics, not just sadness — which is why "just stop thinking about them" fails the same way "just stop craving it" fails an addiction, and why the urge to check their social media or send one more text is a craving spike, not necessarily a signal that reaching out is a good idea.

**When to reach for this:** a wish shaped like "I keep wanting to text them," "why can't I stop checking their Instagram," or "the urge to reach out is unbearable" — name the craving mechanism plainly (it normalizes the intensity without validating acting on it) and set the expectation that this takes real physiological time to fade, the same way a craving curve does, not a single decision.

## Acceptance over avoidance (Hayes' ACT: experiential avoidance)

Hayes' core finding in Acceptance and Commitment Therapy: **experiential avoidance** — the reflexive strategy of trying not to feel the pain (numbing, constant distraction, rebound relationships, overwork) — is the normal, logical first response to psychological pain, and it is also the thing that most reliably prolongs it. The evidence-based alternative is **willingness/acceptance**: letting the feeling be present and move through you rather than fighting it. This is not passive resignation — it's a specific, teachable stance that ACT treats as a primer skill, not a one-time realization.

**When to reach for this:** a wish shaped like "make the pain stop" or "how do I stop feeling this" — the honest Direct Grant is not a distraction plan (that's the avoidance strategy the research says backfires) but something bounded and acceptance-oriented: naming the feeling without judgment, a scheduled time to feel it fully rather than suppressing it all day, or a values-based next action taken *alongside* the pain rather than after it's gone. Pair this with the redirect toward a therapist for anything beyond this bounded scope — ACT is a real clinical modality, and a full course of it is professional territory.

## Self-compassion over rumination (Neff's three components; Lyubomirsky's breakup-specific rumination finding)

Neff's Self-Compassion Scale names three paired components: **self-kindness vs. self-judgment** (treating yourself gently in a hard moment vs. harsh self-criticism), **common humanity vs. isolation** (suffering as something that connects you to others vs. something that isolates you), and **mindfulness vs. rumination** (recognizing the feeling as it is vs. getting lost replaying it). Lyubomirsky's *The How of Happiness* makes the mechanism concrete for this exact wish category: "it may not be the breakup six months ago that's making you depressed but your continued maladaptive ruminations about it" — CBT-based treatment targeting the rumination itself, not just the original event, is well-tested and works.

**When to reach for this:** a wish carrying self-blame ("what's wrong with me," "I ruined the best thing I had," "I'll never find that again") — self-compassion's three components are a more specific, evidence-backed Direct Grant than generic "be kind to yourself" advice, and the mindfulness-vs-rumination axis is the direct lever if the person describes replaying the relationship or the breakup on a loop.

## When it's clinical territory (Prolonged Grief Disorder markers)

DSM-5-TR now recognizes **Prolonged Grief Disorder (PGD)**: grief that remains persistently severe and impairs day-to-day functioning for at least a year (its formal criteria are scoped to bereavement — the death of a loved one — not breakups specifically; treat the severity/duration/impairment markers as a useful analogy for breakup grief, not a diagnosis that formally applies). About 10% of people bereaved by natural causes develop it, at higher rates after sudden, violent, or multiple losses. The evidence is specific and useful for the redirect: the strongest-evidence treatments are short-term psychotherapies combining cognitive components (how the person thinks about the loss and themselves), behavioral components (engaging in a meaningful life again), and meaning-based components (rebuilding a sense of purpose) — tested across multiple randomized trials, and reliably outperforming both supportive counseling and medication alone (one trial found an SSRI no better than placebo for grief severity itself, though it helped co-occurring depression).

**When to reach for this:** this is the concrete backing for "redirect to a therapist" instead of a vague gesture at one — if grief (from death or breakup) has stayed severe and functionally impairing for many months to a year or more, that's the marker for professional support, and the useful detail to pass along is that the person should look for a grief-specific, evidence-based therapy approach (not just general supportive counseling), since the research shows that distinction matters for outcomes.
