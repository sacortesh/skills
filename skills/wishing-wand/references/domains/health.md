# Health & Physical Wellbeing Domain Tenets

Condensed from books actually read and indexed in this environment's library (Anthony Robbins, Roy Baumeister, Anders Ericsson, Gregg Jacobs, Matthew Walker, W. Chris Winter, BJ Fogg, James Clear, Gretchen Rubin). Real survey data (`references/gap-research.md`) puts health/wellbeing at 57.3/100 — the third-highest of six categories — driven mostly by "make my own health a priority" (76.5) rather than a specific number on a scale. This is squarely a Da Rules category 5 domain (sustained-process wish, not an instant grant) — the mechanism is the habit loop in `references/domains/psychology.md`; what follows here is the health-specific content that loop needs to be loaded with.

## Health and fitness are not the same goal (Robbins, *Awaken the Giant Within*)

**Fitness** is the physical capacity to perform athletic activity. **Health** is all the body's systems working well together (nervous, muscular, circulatory, hormonal, etc.). They usually correlate but can diverge — pursuing fitness aggressively at the expense of health (overtraining, ignoring recovery, treating exercise as the entire strategy) is a real failure mode, not a hypothetical one. For a Direct Grant on a health/fitness wish, this is worth naming explicitly if the wish sounds like it's chasing a look or a performance number rather than actual wellbeing — the plan should put health first and let fitness follow, not the reverse.

## Exercise is a keystone habit (Baumeister, *Willpower*)

Research Baumeister cites found that people assigned to a physical-exercise program didn't just get fitter — they also smoked less, drank less, procrastinated less, ate better, and kept their homes cleaner, without those other domains being targeted at all. Building self-control in one area (specifically physical exercise) appears to strengthen it generally. This is a genuinely useful thing to say in a Direct Grant when a health wish is entangled with other stalled goals: starting with exercise specifically (rather than, say, diet alone) is a reasonable default lever because of this spillover effect — though watch for the "licensing effect" Baumeister also documents, where people reward a workout with an unrelated indulgence ("I exercised, so I've earned this"), which quietly cancels the gain.

## Training adaptation requires controlled overload (Ericsson, *Peak*)

The body only changes in response to sustained activity that pushes past its homeostatic comfort zone — moderate, comfortable exercise mostly maintains rather than improves. This is the same "desirable difficulty" principle as `learning.md`'s retrieval-practice tenet, applied to the body instead of the mind: a health/fitness plan that never feels like real effort isn't producing adaptation, and that's worth saying plainly rather than handing over a plan gentle enough to feel comfortable but not actually change anything.

## Insomnia has a behavioral fix before it needs a clinical one (Jacobs, *Say Good Night to Insomnia*; corroborated by Walker, *Why We Sleep*, and Winter, *The Sleep Solution*)

Jacobs's six-week program (developed at Harvard Medical School, built on Richard Bootzin's original stimulus-control research) targets one specific mechanism: the bed has usually become a learned cue for wakefulness — lying awake, working, scrolling, watching TV — instead of a cue for sleep, and that association can be retrained the same way any cue-routine link can. **Stimulus control**, the core technique: use the bed for sleep (and sex) only; go to bed only when actually sleepy, not just tired-bored; if not asleep within about twenty minutes, get up and do something quiet and relaxing in dim light until drowsiness returns, then go back — repeating as many times as needed; keep a consistent wake time every day regardless of how the night went; remove visible clocks from view (clock-watching itself generates the arousal that prevents sleep). Winter's reframe of the companion technique, sleep restriction, is worth carrying into a Direct Grant verbatim because it kills the misleading name: it isn't restricting sleep, it's restricting *time spent in bed not sleeping* — cutting the window down to roughly the amount of sleep actually being gotten, so sleep pressure rebuilds and the bed re-associates with sleeping rather than tossing. Walker's independent list of "obvious" fixes converges on the same behavioral territory rather than contradicting it: fixed sleep/wake time including weekends, no napping when nights are already bad, cutting caffeine and alcohol, no bedroom screens, a cool dark room, and — the one easy to miss — deliberately winding down mentally before bed rather than expecting the mind to stop on command.

**When to reach for this:** wishes like "I want to fix my sleep schedule," "I can't fall asleep," "I want to actually feel rested," where the person hasn't been diagnosed with anything — stimulus control plus a consistent wake time is a legitimate, well-evidenced Direct Grant on its own. **The category 4 line stays explicit:** this is the self-administrable slice of what full CBT-I (cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia) does with a therapist over several weeks; Jacobs's own program is the source that documents this. What this file will not do is diagnose "insomnia" as a clinical entity, address what Winter calls "hard" chronic insomnia that hasn't responded to the basics, or touch a suspected physiological sleep disorder (apnea, restless leg, etc.) — those need a sleep clinician, not a wand.

## Diets fail from a structural trap, not a character flaw (Baumeister, *Willpower*; Fogg, *Tiny Habits*; supplemented by Clear, *Atomic Habits*, and Rubin, *Better Than Before*)

Baumeister names the actual mechanical bind under most failed diets, which he calls the dieter's catch-22: resisting food costs willpower, and willpower is a finite resource that runs on glucose — so the very act of resisting eating depletes the resource needed to keep resisting, and a long enough stretch of resistance reliably ends in "I already broke it, might as well" collapse. This is not a willpower-strength problem to be solved by trying harder; it's why Fogg's answer (in the *Tiny Habits* "SuperFridge" technique) is to stop asking willpower to do the job at all and instead redesign the food environment so the easy default is the wanted one — stocking the fridge and pantry so what's visible and reachable is what should get eaten, rather than staging a fresh willpower contest at every meal. This is the same cue-based lever as this file's cross-reference, `psychology.md`'s habit-loop section (cue → craving → routine → reward) — Clear's research corroborates it specifically for food: what gets eaten responds heavily to contextual cues like visibility and proximity, largely independent of actual physiological hunger, which is why "just have more willpower around food" fights the wrong variable. Rubin's *Better Than Before* adds a second, distinct lever worth naming alongside environment design: **monitoring** — bigger packages and multi-bite-sized servings (dim sum, tapas, hors d'oeuvres) make consumption reliably harder to track, and the fix is making consumption visible again (saving wrappers, using smaller packaging, writing it down) rather than trusting felt self-control to catch it.

**When to reach for this:** wishes like "I want to eat healthier," "I want to lose weight," "I keep breaking my diet" — the Direct Grant is environment redesign (what's visible/reachable) plus a monitoring mechanism, explicitly *not* a bigger-willpower pep talk, which the Baumeister mechanism shows is fighting the wrong battle. **The category 4 line stays explicit:** this is nutrition-*behavior*, not nutrition-*science* — no calorie targets, macro splits, or "what should I actually eat" content belongs here, and if a wish's details suggest disordered eating (binging, purging, extreme restriction) rather than ordinary diet-adherence struggle, that is clinical territory for a professional, not this file.

## Known gap

Clinical sleep disorders (chronic/"hard" insomnia, apnea, and other physiological sleep pathology), diagnosable eating disorders, and live dietary/macro guidance remain out of scope — squarely Da Rules category 4 territory where medical specifics need a professional, not this skill. What's now covered: the behavioral, non-clinical layer of sleep hygiene (stimulus control, sleep restriction reframed) and diet-adherence behavior (environment redesign, monitoring) — the same behavioral register as this file's existing exercise-and-willpower content, not a clinical upgrade.
