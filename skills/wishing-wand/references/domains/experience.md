# Experience Domain Tenets

Real survey data (`references/gap-research.md`) found "experience" wishes (travel, new things, hobbies, following a passion, mastery) score *highest* of all six major categories (64.3/100) — this is the most common wish-shape this skill will see, and it had zero dedicated content before this pass. It's also the category most entangled with other domains, so this file is mostly a router plus a couple of genuinely load-bearing tenets of its own (passion-as-leisure, and hobby-selection choice overload).

## "Follow my passion" is a career.md problem wearing an experience.md costume

The survey's actual top experience wishes are "experience new things," "pursue hobbies/interests," "travel," and "follow my passion" — that last one is the same wish `career.md` already covers in depth: Cal Newport's finding that passion follows mastery rather than preceding it. If the wish is about passion in the context of work/career, use `career.md`. If it's about passion/interest as a leisure pursuit with no career stakes (a hobby, a creative practice, a sport), the same underlying mechanic still applies — competence and engagement typically build in that order, so a Direct Grant for "I want to find something I'm passionate about" is better shaped as "commit to getting genuinely competent at one specific candidate thing for a real stretch of time" than as an open-ended exploration exercise. `learning.md`'s retrieval-and-directness mechanics are what actually build that competence.

## "Hobby hopping" and decision paralysis are a choice-overload problem, not a character flaw

Barry Schwartz's maximizer/satisficer distinction is the mechanism behind "I can't commit to a hobby" or "too many options, I keep starting and dropping things" — and it isn't a single author's pop-psych opinion here: it's independently quoted and treated as settled research across at least four separately-indexed books (Haidt's *The Happiness Hypothesis*, Rubin's *The Happiness Project*, Seligman's *Authentic Happiness*, Baumeister's *Willpower*), which is stronger corroboration than this skill usually gets for a secondhand source. Maximizers keep evaluating every candidate looking for the objectively best one; satisficers stop at "good enough" and commit. Maximizers end up *less* satisfied and more prone to regret than satisficers, even though they make marginally better picks on average — more options and more searching make the outcome worse, not better. The counterintuitive Direct Grant for a hobby-commitment wish is therefore the opposite of what feels helpful: don't hand over more options or a bigger exploration phase (that's the maximizer trap restated) — artificially narrow the field to one candidate and commit to a bounded trial.

Eve Rodsky's *Fair Play* gives a concrete version of that narrowing move ("Unicorn Space"): pick one of a small number of buckets — hands (making/building/crafting), heart-connection (volunteering, group/social activity), heart-adrenaline (physical/adventure), head (skill, intellectual, or purpose-driven) — drill down to one specific candidate inside it, and commit for a bounded window (Rodsky uses six months) anchored to a concrete, dated, externally-visible goal (a small performance, a demo, a deadline someone else is expecting to see), not an open-ended "try it and see how it feels." The externally-visible deadline is what converts "explore this hobby" from a private intention — easy to quietly abandon the moment novelty fades — into a real commitment with stakes attached.

One honest caveat: not all hobby-switching is a problem this mechanic should fix. Frequent switching is also linked (in current, non-book, lower-tier reporting — not this file's usual book-grade bar, so weight it as a caveat, not a tenet) to ADHD-driven novelty-seeking and dopamine-chasing, and some psychologists frame it as a legitimate way to regulate attention or explore identity rather than a discipline failure. If a wish is phrased as "I keep abandoning hobbies," check whether the actual ask is the Fair-Play-style commitment mechanic above, or reassurance that switching is fine — those are different wishes wearing identical words, and forcing the commitment mechanic onto the second one is a bad grant.

## Travel/bucket-list wishes are usually resource-shaped, not motivation-shaped

Unlike habit-shaped wishes (gaming time, fitness, learning), "tick off my travel bucket list" rarely fails because of insufficient desire — it fails on the resource-gap axis (time off, money, logistics) or on "waiting to feel ready" (per `references/gap-research.md`'s barrier data, 20.1% of people cite exactly this as a barrier to any wish). Treat this as a plan-shaped Direct Grant problem: name the specific first trip, the actual constraint (money, PTO, a travel companion), and a concrete next action — not a "someday" list restated back.

## "Become less materialistic" is the odd one out

This is the one experience-category item that isn't really about doing more — it's a values/identity shift, and it scores notably lower (50.7) than the other items in its own category, suggesting it's a less-felt or less-clear wish than "travel" or "follow my passion." If someone wishes for this specifically, it likely pairs with `finance.md`'s Housel material (savings as the ego-income gap) or `psychology.md`'s Branden material (self-acceptance not dependent on external proof) rather than needing its own framework — don't invent a dedicated "minimalism" mechanism when the real driver is one of those two.

## Known gap

Hobby-selection paralysis and "hobby hopping" are covered above (Schwartz's maximizer/satisficer mechanism, corroborated across four indexed books, plus Fair Play's Unicorn Space as a concrete narrowing method) — checked specifically per this skill's gap list and confirmed as a genuine, distinct mechanism worth having natively rather than routing away.

Trip-planning logistics specifically stayed thin on purpose: a RAG search turned up nothing decision-usable beyond what's already in the travel/bucket-list tenet above (S.J. Scott's *Level Up Your Day* just restates "break the trip into concrete steps," which the existing tenet already covers) and a pile of irrelevant AI-agent-architecture material that happens to use "travel planning" as a worked example. No indexed book offers an actual trip-planning framework (route optimization, timing, budget allocation) beyond generic advice a live web search already covers better with current prices and options — so, like the parenting and spiritual/religious gaps handled elsewhere in this skill, this specific sub-gap is being left as a documented non-gap rather than forced. Same verdict for bucket-list *structuring* beyond "name the first trip and a concrete next action," which the existing tenet already states.
