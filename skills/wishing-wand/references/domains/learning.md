# Learning Domain Tenets

Condensed from books actually read and indexed in this environment's library (Scott Young, Brown/McDaniel/Roediger, Benny Lewis). Use these to make a learning-shaped wish's Direct Grant (or the brief handed to `learning-aspire`, see `routing-guide.md`) concrete instead of generic ("practice daily!") — and note this domain overlaps with `learning-aspire`, which already exists to carry a learning goal across sessions; these tenets are what should inform *what that skill's Source/Probe/Ingest phases actually contain*, not a reason to write a competing one-off plan.

## The core mechanism: retrieval beats review

Brown, McDaniel & Roediger's central, heavily-evidenced finding: **actively recalling** information (testing yourself) embeds it far more durably than re-reading or re-watching it, even though re-reading *feels* more productive in the moment. The effortful version — spaced out, not crammed, hard enough that you sometimes fail to recall — produces the most durable learning ("desirable difficulty," per Bjork). Practical implication for any learning-shaped wish: the plan needs a forced self-test built in, not just more exposure to material. This is exactly the gap most people's failed attempts have — endless input, no retrieval — and it's why `learning-aspire`'s explicit Retrieve step exists rather than ending at Ingest.

## Scott Young's Ultralearning principles (the other half of the mechanism)

Two of the nine principles matter most for shaping a Direct Grant or a `learning-aspire` brief:

- **Metalearning first** — before diving in, spend a small amount of time mapping how the skill/subject actually breaks down and how others have learned it, rather than improvising a curriculum from scratch. A brief map prevents wasted effort on the wrong sub-skills.
- **Directness, then drill** — practice in the actual target context as early as possible (speak the language, write the code, give the talk), and only isolate a specific weak sub-skill for focused drilling *after* direct practice reveals what's actually weak. Practicing components in isolation before ever using them directly is the classic way academic-style study plans fail to transfer to real use.

## Language learning specifically (maps directly onto "learn Spanish" -type wishes)

Benny Lewis's one load-bearing rule: **speak from day one** — don't wait for a vague "ready" threshold before using the language with a real person. Waiting to feel prepared is itself the failure mode, not a reasonable precaution. Combined with the retrieval principle above: the single highest-leverage habit swap for a "I keep quitting" language wish is trading passive study (apps, flashcards alone) for early, frequent, imperfect real use — a fast path to a feedback loop, which is exactly the mechanism a stalled attempt was missing.

## What this means for routing

When a wish is "I want to learn X and actually stick with it," the honest read (per Da Rules category 5 and the actionability axis) is that the missing piece is almost never content — it's retrieval, spacing, and real-context practice sustained past the motivation-honeymoon period. That's precisely what `learning-aspire`'s Probe → Ingest → **Retrieve → Endure** loop is structured to provide across sessions. Dispatch there for anything that needs to survive more than a week; reserve a one-off Direct Grant here for someone who just wants the *content* map for a short, bounded task (e.g. "what should I read to get oriented on X this weekend"), not a sustained-fluency goal.
