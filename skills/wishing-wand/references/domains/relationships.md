# Relationships Domain Tenets

Condensed from books actually read and indexed in this environment's library — Esther Perel and Jack Morin for the romantic/intimacy section below, and Marisa Franco (*Platonic*, 870 chunks), Karl Pillemer (*Fault Lines*, 750 chunks), and Joshua Coleman (*Rules of Estrangement*, 722 chunks) for the friendship and family-estrangement sections, all now directly sourced from the indexed text rather than secondhand summaries. This file exists because of the single biggest gap this skill had: a real 5,000-person survey (Real Insurance/CoreData, 2020 — see `references/gap-research.md`) found "family and friends" is tied for the *highest*-scoring wish category people report (index 62.0/100, behind only "experience" at 64.3), driven overwhelmingly by wanting more time with loved ones, finding/keeping love, staying in touch with old friends (55.1%), and reconciling with someone (30.5%) — yet this skill originally had no domain content for it at all beyond the grief/loss Da Rules entry.

## Romantic/intimacy-shaped wishes ("I want to fall in love again," "I want more passion in my relationship")

The load-bearing finding across both Perel (*Mating in Captivity*) and Morin (*The Erotic Mind*): **closeness and desire pull in different directions**. Perel's framing: love needs both surrender (togetherness) and autonomy (separateness) — "too much distance, no connection; too much merging, no one left to connect with." Long-term couples who successfully build compatibility and closeness often see erotic charge decline *because* of that same closeness, not despite it — the qualities that make a relationship stable (predictability, merging, complementarity) are frequently the same qualities that dull desire, which itself thrives on some distance/mystery/autonomy.

Practical implication for a Direct Grant: a wish like "I want more passion/excitement in my relationship" is usually *not* solved by more togetherness or more "date nights that talk about the relationship" — it's more often solved by reintroducing autonomy/distance/separateness (individual pursuits, novelty, not-fully-knowing) without threatening the underlying security. A wish like "I want a deeper connection" is the opposite lever: more self-disclosure, more shared vulnerability, more of the "closeness emotions" (Morin: love, tenderness, connection, appreciation). Diagnose which one the wish is actually asking for before answering — they're not the same problem and the fix for one can actively hurt the other.

## Family/friends time-shaped wishes ("I want to spend more time with people I love")

The survey data says this is usually not a scheduling problem alone — "keep the family happy and together" and "spend more time with loved ones" score higher than almost anything else measured, across every generation. Treat this as a resource-gap-axis case first (`intent-axes.md`): is time genuinely scarce (structural — a job, distance, a caregiving load), or is it a priority/habit problem (time exists but isn't protected)? The habit-loop mechanics in `references/domains/psychology.md` (implementation intentions, habit stacking) apply directly here — "call a parent every Sunday at 6pm" beats "spend more time with family" as an actual plan.

## Reconciliation wishes ("I want to fix things with X")

Distinct from the grief/loss Da Rules category (which covers wanting someone back who doesn't want contact) — a reconciliation wish where the other party is reachable and the relationship isn't definitively over is closer to Da Rules category 2 (can't control another person's choice) crossed with the NVC four-part structure in `psychology.md`: draft the observation/feeling/need/request, don't draft a persuasion script.

## Friendship-maintenance wishes ("I want to stay close with my old friends," "I want to make more friends as an adult")

Real survey data anchors this as a top regret, not a niche complaint: 55.1% of Real Wishes Report respondents wish they'd stayed in touch with old friends, and "wishing I'd stayed in touch with friends" independently shows up as Ware's #4 hospice regret (`gap-research.md`) — two unrelated datasets converging on the same gap. Marisa Franco's *Platonic: How the Science of Attachment Can Help You Make — and Keep — Friends* (Dr. Marisa G. Franco, PhD; 2022, Penguin) supplies the load-bearing mechanism, and the indexed text confirms and sharpens it: "Adult Friendships Don't Happen Organically" (Chapter 3, "Taking Initiative") — "making friends as an adult requires initiative. We have to put ourselves out there and try. It's a process of reaching out over and over again." The mechanism isn't just attachment style — the indexed text's strongest empirical anchor is a study Franco cites (Nancy Newall et al., Brandon University) of older adults tracked over five years: those who believed friendship happens by luck/organically got *lonelier*, while those who believed it takes effort did not, because that belief predicted actually doing the social activities (visiting people, joining groups) that produce friends. Franco names this an internal vs. external "locus of control," and frames attachment style as a second, compounding layer on top of it: anxious attachment ("assume others will abandon them... plunge into intimacy too rapidly") and avoidant attachment ("keep others at a distance... push others away, eschew vulnerability, and leave relationships prematurely") each supply a different reason to under-initiate — so the correction here is that belief-about-effort, not attachment style alone, is the mechanism Franco's own cited evidence weighs most heavily.

Practical implication for a Direct Grant: a wish like "I want to stay close with my old friends" is rarely solved by waiting for a natural moment — the plan is a low-effort, recurring initiation that doesn't depend on the other person reciprocating in kind to keep running. The book's own worked example (quoting Kat Vellos, *We Should Get Together*) is exactly this pattern: "A basic but critical part of nurturing relationships is the act of following up and checking in with people. I scheduled repeating reminders in my phone to reach out both to my old friends and new friends" — i.e., "text every Sunday," "annual trip," "standing monthly call" isn't an invented Direct Grant template, it's the book's literal recommended tactic, borrowing the same implementation-intention mechanics as habit-shaped wishes (`psychology.md`). A wish like "I want to make new friends as an adult" needs a different lever: a recurring-proximity substitute (a class, league, or group that meets on a schedule) — Franco's own example is introducing yourself to neighbors and turning it into a standing weekly picnic — since both Franco and the Harvard Study of Adult Development (cited in `references/gap-research.md`'s source material and Nir Eyal's *Indistractable*, which is indexed here) converge on *frequency of contact*, not compatibility, as the actual bottleneck once someone's past the age where an institution hands proximity to them for free.

## Family estrangement/reconciliation wishes ("I want to fix things with my [family member] we haven't spoken to in years," "I want to end an estrangement")

Distinct from the reconciliation entry above (which assumes the relationship has friction but is still active) — this is for a full or near-full cutoff, and the data says it's common, not rare: Karl Pillemer's *Fault Lines: Fractured Families and How to Mend Them* (2021, Cornell Family Estrangement & Reconciliation Project national survey) found 27% of Americans currently estranged from a relative (~67 million people) — and the indexed text is explicit that this is a *floor*, not a central estimate: "the actual prevalence of estrangement is likely higher, as some survey respondents may have not wished to admit the problem." Filtering to people who said the rift actually *upsets* them (removing the "we just lost touch" cases) only drops the number from 27% to 22% (~55 million) — and of those, 85% had been estranged a year or more, half for four years or more. Two directly-indexed sources converge on reconciliation mechanics specifically — Pillemer's *Fault Lines* (national survey plus in-depth interviews with people who achieved reconciliation) and Joshua Coleman's *Rules of Estrangement* (2021; clinical psychologist, 40 years of practice, drawing partly on his own reconciliation with his estranged daughter).

The load-bearing, counterintuitive finding from Pillemer's successful-reconciler interviews, Chapter 7 ("Let Go of the Past"): almost all of them had abandoned the need for the estranged relative to accept their version of events or apologize first. The book states the tool kit as three explicit strategies, nearly verbatim: "Bridging the rift requires abandoning the urge to align the past," "Time spent waiting for an apology is time wasted," and "Focus on building a new future that can eclipse the past." A paired mechanism from Chapter 8 ("Taking Responsibility"): "taking a step back and examining one's own role is transformative... acknowledging shared responsibility was liberating and empowering, whether or not the relationship was restored" — i.e., self-examination pays off even if reconciliation never happens, which is a stronger and more specific claim than the general "treat the rift as two-way" framing. One nuance the web-summary version missed: dropping the demand for a shared narrative does *not* mean offering a vague, blanket apology — Pillemer's Chapter 10 ("Setting Boundaries") warns that "a willingness to 'apologize for anything' is not much of an apology at all" and that successful reconciliation bids are specific ("I promise not to bring up X again," not "I'm sorry for whatever is bothering you").

Coleman's complementary mechanism for the person reaching out, from Chapter 11: a "letter of amends" that takes responsibility for concrete, specific harm without conditioning it on the other person's response. The indexed text is sharper than the original summary on *why* letters fail — Coleman's #1 most common mistake is not going far enough, i.e., writing "I'm sorry you felt that I was controlling" (a non-apology that blames the other person's perception) instead of "Yes, I can be a pretty controlling person. I could see how that might have been hard on you" (owning the behavior directly). This pairs with a posture shift Coleman states as explicit rules (Chapter 12, "Ten New Rules for Parent–Adult Child Relations"): Rule #1, "Your adult child has more power than you to set the terms of your relationship because they're more willing to walk away" — basic game theory, "she who cares less has more power"; Rule #3, "You are not the only authority on how well you performed as a parent. Your adult child gets to have their own narrative and opinions about the past" (which directly reinforces Pillemer's "drop the shared-narrative demand" finding from the parent's side too); and Rule #5, that egalitarian, psychological, self-aware communication has replaced automatic parental authority — Coleman frames this as a societal shift where "respect isn't given, it's earned" has replaced "honor thy mother and father," so an appeal to authority or "I'm still your parent" reliably backfires.

Practical implication for a Direct Grant: this wish is not usually served by helping someone draft the strongest case for their own side of the history — the actual research finding is that dropping the precondition that the other party agree on the past is what correlates with repair, not winning the argument about it. But it also isn't served by a vague, blanket apology — both Pillemer and Coleman independently found that specificity (naming the exact behavior, the exact change being offered) is what makes an outreach attempt land, where sweeping non-apologies don't. The NVC four-part structure (`psychology.md`) still supplies the *wording* of an outreach attempt once the shared-narrative precondition is dropped, but the letter/message itself should name concrete specifics, not generalities. If the wish is closer to "I want peace even if reconciliation never happens" than to active repair, redirect to Da Rules category 2 (can't control another person's choice) rather than treating it as a reconciliation-plan wish — and note that this is also directly supported by Pillemer's data: self-examination of one's own role was described as valuable "whether or not the relationship was restored."

## Known gap

This file now covers plain friendship-maintenance and family-estrangement reconciliation specifically (previous gap), and as of this pass all three sources (Franco's *Platonic*, Pillemer's *Fault Lines*, Coleman's *Rules of Estrangement*) are indexed in this environment's library and directly sourced from the text, same as Perel/Morin — no more secondhand-summary caveat needed. Still thin on: sibling-specific estrangement dynamics — Pillemer's survey puts sibling estrangement at 8% of the population (~20 million) versus 10% for parent/child (~25 million), so siblings are nearly as common a case as parent/child, but this file doesn't yet separate Pillemer's sibling-specific mechanisms from the parent/child ones used above; in-law and blended-family conflict specifically (Coleman devotes a chapter to in-law dynamics that this file hasn't mined yet); and the acute/abusive end of estrangement (where reconciliation may not be the right goal at all and a professional referral, not a Direct Grant, is the right move — this file's mechanisms assume a repairable rift, not an abusive one; Coleman himself is explicit that some parents "leave no other option" for the adult child "than to walk").
